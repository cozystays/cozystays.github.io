Val Gardena & Alta Badia

Three villages up one South Tyrolean valley, a fourth over the pass, and the best-connected lift network in the Alps wrapped around them. Also the place where picking the wrong village costs you an hour a day.

Which village to stay in

Val Gardena is three villages in a row, each a little higher than the last. They are not interchangeable.

Ortisei, the town one

The largest and lowest at about 1,236 m, and the one that feels like somewhere people actually live — a pedestrian centre, shops, the valley's woodcarving tradition still visible in the workshops. The trade-off is the skiing: Ortisei connects to the main area less directly than the two villages above it, so you spend more of each morning getting to where you are going.

Pick it if you want a place with a life of its own and you are happy to lose a little ski time.

Santa Cristina, the middle one

At about 1,428 m, smaller and quieter than Ortisei, better placed than Ortisei for the lifts. The sensible compromise, and usually the cheapest of the three.

Selva, the ski one

Highest of the three at about 1,563 m and squarely built around skiing. The Sella Ronda starts here, lifts leave from the village, and the atmosphere is a ski resort rather than a town. Pick it if the skiing is the point and the evenings are a bonus.

Corvara, over in Alta Badia

About 1,568 m, roughly 45 minutes from Val Gardena by road, and a different valley with a different feel. Around 91% of residents speak Ladin as their first language — older than both Italian and German here — and the food is a genuine reason to come: several restaurants in the valley carry Michelin recognition, which is unusual for a ski area of this size.

The Sella Ronda, honestly

The Sella Ronda is a circuit of lifts and pistes that takes you right around the Sella massif — roughly 40 km, about fifteen lifts, five to six hours if it goes well. You can ride it clockwise (green) or anticlockwise (orange). It is the thing most people come here to do once.

What the brochures leave out:

  • Start early. The queues build at the entry lifts between roughly 9:30 and 10:30. An hour's head start changes the whole day.
  • It is a schedule, not a ski run. Miss the last lift on a section and you are off the circuit, in the wrong valley, paying for a long taxi back around the mountain.
  • You need the full Dolomiti Superski pass, not a single-area one. That pass covers about 1,200 km across twelve areas, which is the real argument for this corner of the Alps.
  • In summer the same circuit is a well-known mountain bike route.

Getting there without a car

Easier than the Aosta Valley, harder than it looks on a map. There is no station in the valley. The railway gets you to Bolzano (or Brixen), and from there it is a bus up — about an hour from Bolzano.

By air, Verona is the usual choice at roughly 110 to 190 km depending on which route you take, which is about two and a half to three hours by road. Venice is further, around three and a half to four hours. Innsbruck is closer in kilometres but involves crossing the Brenner and three to four hours with changes by public transport.

One thing worth planning around: Saturday is changeover day across the whole region, and the valley roads can add an hour or more of queuing in winter. A midweek arrival is a different experience from a Saturday one.

Booking the practical bits

  • Private transfer Verona airport → Selva di Val Gardena — fixed price agreed in advance, driver waiting at arrivals, ski bags included rather than wrestled onto a bus.
  • Private transfer Verona airport → Ortisei — same road, the first of the three villages you reach.
  • An eSIM for Italy — South Tyrol is close enough to the Austrian border that phones sometimes wander onto an Austrian network. Worth sorting before you land.
  • If your flight is delayed or cancelled — what EU rules pay, and what they do not.

Once you are in the valley the lifts themselves connect the villages, which is why so few people here bother with a car.

Travelling with children

This is one of the better Alpine regions for families, mostly because the villages are real places rather than lift stations — Ortisei in particular has somewhere to go when the weather turns. South Tyrol is bilingual, German and Italian with Ladin on top, and English is widely spoken in ski schools, which removes a worry.

Two cautions. The Sella Ronda is not a family outing: six hours and a hard deadline on the last lifts is a lot to ask of a child, and being stranded in the wrong valley with one is a bad afternoon. And the transfer from Verona is two and a half hours or more, so an early flight is worth the money.

The honest drawbacks

It is busy. This is one of the most popular ski regions in the Alps, and in February it feels like it. It is heavily reliant on snowmaking — over 90% of the pistes here are covered by snow cannons, which keeps the season running but means early-season skiing can be white ribbons through green fields.

And for strong skiers the terrain is more extensive than it is difficult. The Dolomiti Superski pass buys an enormous amount of piste; it does not buy a great deal of genuinely steep skiing.

Is it worth it in summer?

Arguably more than in winter, and with a fraction of the crowds. Hiking and via ferrata across the Dolomites, climbing around Corvara, paragliding, and the Sella Ronda ridden on a mountain bike instead of skis. Val Gardena's woodcarving workshops stay open year-round and are a genuine local craft rather than a souvenir operation.
