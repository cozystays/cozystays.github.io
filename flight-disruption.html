Before you fly

Flight delayed or cancelled

Winter is the season for it. Here is what the rules actually pay, when they pay nothing at all, and why claiming afterwards and insuring beforehand are two different products.

What EU rules pay

If your flight departs from an EU airport, or lands in the EU on an EU airline, Regulation 261/2004 sets fixed compensation by the distance of the flight — not by what your ticket cost. A 49 euro fare and a 400 euro fare on the same route are owed the same amount.

  • Up to 1,500 km: up to €250. This is the band almost every flight from Germany, Switzerland or Austria to Italy falls into — Zurich to Rome, Munich to Naples, Berlin to Milan.
  • 1,500 to 3,500 km: up to €400.
  • Over 3,500 km: up to €600.

The trigger is the delay on arrival, not departure, and the threshold is three hours. A flight that pushes back two hours late and makes most of it up in the air pays nothing. Cancellation announced less than fourteen days ahead is treated the same way as a long delay.

When you get nothing

This is the part most articles skip, and it is the reason a lot of claims fail:

  • Extraordinary circumstances. Weather, air traffic control strikes, security threats, a medical emergency on board. The airline does not pay for these, and that covers a large share of winter disruption.
  • A re-route that gets you there in time. If the airline puts you on another flight that arrives within the allowed window, the compensation shrinks or disappears.
  • You were not at check-in. No-shows on a flight that was delayed rather than cancelled are not owed anything.
  • Someone already claimed it. One claim per passenger per booking reference — a second one is closed as a duplicate.

So treat the compensation as a possibility, not a budget item. And be aware that airline compensation takes months to resolve even when the claim is good.

Claiming afterwards

If a flight has already gone wrong — this trip or an earlier one — you file a claim and a service chases the airline for you, taking a cut only if money arrives. Nothing to pay up front. The catch is time: moving a claim to its final status commonly takes three to six months, and longer if it goes to court.

Insuring beforehand

The other approach solves the thing EU261 does not: the day itself. You are in the terminal, the board says four hours, and a payout that may arrive next spring is no help at all.

A flight-disruption membership flips that around. You register each flight at least 48 hours before departure, and the cover is on the clock rather than on a court case:

  • Lounge access once the delay passes one hour — over 1,300 airports.

What to do at the airport

Whatever you have bought, the same four things decide whether a later claim holds up:

  • Photograph the departure board showing your flight and the delay.
  • Keep the boarding pass and the booking reference — a claim without the reference goes nowhere.
  • Ask the airline desk, in writing if you can, for the reason for the delay. "Operational" is not the same as weather, and the distinction is exactly what the claim turns on.
  • Keep receipts for food and a hotel. Those are owed under a separate duty of care that applies even when compensation does not.

Also worth sorting before you fly

  • Compare fares across airlines — whether Rome, Milan or Naples is the cheaper way in changes by season.
  • An eSIM for Italy — set up before departure, working the moment you land. Useful precisely on the day something goes wrong and you need to rebook from the gate.
  • A fixed-price transfer from the airport — the driver tracks the flight and waits, which matters more on a delayed arrival than on a punctual one.
