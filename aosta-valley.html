Winter region

Aosta Valley

Italy's north-western corner, wedged against France and Switzerland. Three very different bases under the Mont Blanc and Matterhorn, and the one Italian region where you can ski across an international border before lunch.

Which base to pick

Courmayeur, if you want a real town

At about 1,224 m, Courmayeur is an actual Alpine town rather than a purpose-built resort — a car-free old centre, proper restaurants, and the Mont Blanc filling the sky behind it. The thing nobody tells you: the gondola does not leave from the centre. It starts in Dolonne, a separate part of town, so wherever you sleep you will be walking or taking the local bus to the lift each morning.

It also sits low for an Alpine base. In a mild week it can rain in the town while it snows on the slopes.

Breuil-Cervinia, if you want altitude

Cervinia is the opposite: a purpose-built resort at about 2,050 m, right under the Italian side of the Matterhorn, with glacier skiing above it. It is not pretty in the way Courmayeur is — it was built for the snow, not for the architecture — but it is high, and in a poor season that matters more than charm.

La Thuile, if you want it quiet

The smallest of the three at about 1,441 m, and the least known outside Italy. Its ski area connects to La Rosière across the French border. Note that the lifts start a short way out of the village, roughly 800 m, so again: check where your room sits relative to the lift, not relative to the village centre.

The border crossing that makes Cervinia worth it

From Cervinia you can ski over to Zermatt in Switzerland. The crossing runs over the glacier at Plateau Rosa, with the border at around 3,480 m — you go up on the Italian side and come down into another country. It needs the international ski pass rather than the local one, and it needs weather: if the top lifts are shut, the crossing is shut, and you are skiing Cervinia's own slopes instead.

Plan it for the middle of the week, not the last day. If the weather closes in on the Swiss side while you are over there, getting back is a long taxi around the mountain.

Getting there without a car

This is where the Aosta Valley is harder work than the Dolomites. There is no railway to Courmayeur. The line up the valley ends at Aosta, and from there it is a bus — roughly hourly. For Cervinia, the nearest station is Châtillon-Saint-Vincent down in the main valley, then a bus up.

So the realistic options from Milan are: train to Aosta or Châtillon plus a bus with ski luggage and a change, or a door-to-door transfer booked in advance.

Booking the practical bits

  • Private transfer Malpensa → Courmayeur — roughly 2 h 15 by road. Fixed price agreed in advance, driver meets you at arrivals, and the car takes the ski bags that the bus will not thank you for.
  • Private transfer Malpensa → Breuil-Cervinia — roughly 2 h 15. The alternative is train plus a valley bus with a change at Châtillon.
  • An eSIM for Italy — set up before you fly. Lift status and weather pages are the two things you will check every morning.
  • If your flight is delayed or cancelled — what EU rules pay, and what they do not.

Once you are up there, all three resorts run local buses to the lifts, so you do not need a car for the week — only to get in and out.

Travelling with children

Courmayeur is the easiest of the three with children, because it is a town: there is somewhere to go in the evening, there are shops and a pharmacy, and a day off the slopes is not a day staring at a wall. The thermal baths at Pré-Saint-Didier are a short bus ride away and work well as a weather day.

Cervinia suits families who want to ski and little else — everything is close together and the nursery slopes sit right by the village, but there is not much to do once the lifts stop. La Thuile is quiet enough that older children may be bored and young ones perfectly happy.

One practical point for all three: the transfer from Milan is two hours or more. With small children, an afternoon flight means arriving in the dark on a mountain road. An early flight is worth paying for.

What is wrong with each one

Written down honestly, because every resort page on the internet reads the same otherwise.

Courmayeur has limited beginner terrain and few genuinely hard runs. Strong skiers tend to find they have seen the area inside a day or two, and then they are paying mountain prices to repeat it. It is the right base if you want a town, mixed days and good food, and the wrong base if you want to ski hard all week.

Cervinia is high and snow-sure but limited at the top end — advanced skiers run out of challenge quickly, and the resort itself has no soul to fall back on. It is at its best for intermediates who want long, open, cruising runs.

La Thuile is the least documented of the three. The resort's own material talks up its microclimate; independent snow records are harder to come by. Treat the snow-reliability claims as marketing until you have checked a live report.

Is it worth it in summer?

Courmayeur is the one that works year-round — hiking and mountaineering under the Mont Blanc, the Skyway cable car up to 3,466 m, and the Pré-Saint-Didier baths. Cervinia keeps a summer ski season on the glacier, roughly late June to early September, which is a niche but a real one. In high summer the valley is markedly cooler than the Italian lakes an hour south, which is its own argument.

Also worth sorting

  • Compare fares across airlines — Milan Malpensa and Turin are both plausible ways in; Turin is closer by road.
  • Left luggage in Milan — useful if you build a city night into either end of the trip rather than transferring straight through.
