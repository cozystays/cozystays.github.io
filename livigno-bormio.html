Livigno & Bormio

Two valleys in the far north of Lombardy, an hour apart and not much alike. One is a high, duty-free strip of a town; the other is a spa town with Roman baths and an Olympic downhill.

Livigno: high, long and tax-free

Livigno sits at about 1,816 m with roughly 6,800 residents, strung out along the valley floor for several kilometres. That shape is the single most important thing to know before you book: where you stay decides your whole week. There is no compact centre with everything around it. A room at the wrong end means the bus to the lifts every morning and back every evening.

The skiing is split across two sectors, Carosello 3000 on one side and Mottolino on the other, with no lift between them. A free bus links them, which works, but it means a deliberate decision each morning rather than skiing from one to the other.

The duty-free part is real. Livigno has been exempt from Italian customs duty since around 1840 and sits outside the normal VAT regime, so fuel, alcohol, tobacco, electronics and ski gear are noticeably cheaper than elsewhere in Italy. There are quantity limits on what you may take out, which matters if you plan to stock up.

Bormio: lower, older, with hot water

Bormio is down at about 1,225 m and is a proper old town with a medieval core, not a ski development. Its thermal baths have been in use since Roman times, and they are the reason a week in Bormio works even for people who do not ski every day.

The town hosted the men's alpine events of the 2026 Winter Olympics, which brought investment and attention. Its ski area reaches about 3,012 m with roughly 1,800 m of vertical in one go — the sort of top-to-bottom run that is rarer than resort maps suggest.

Published figures for Bormio's own pisted kilometres vary a good deal between sources, and combined figures that fold in Santa Caterina and San Colombano vary more. Check the current lift company's own map rather than a comparison site.

Getting there without a car

Neither valley has a railway. For both, the nearest station is Tirano, which is the end of the line up the Valtellina, and from there it is a bus up the mountain. Livigno can also be reached from the Swiss side via Zernez.

The distances are longer than people expect. Livigno is about 196 km from Bergamo and roughly four hours by road from Milan — this is not a resort you reach on an afternoon flight. Only three roads lead into the valley, and the Italian approach climbs over the Foscagno pass at about 2,291 m, which is exactly the kind of road you do not want to meet for the first time in a hire car, in the dark, in snow.

Booking the practical bits

  • Private transfer Malpensa → Livigno — the long one, over the Foscagno pass. Fixed price agreed in advance and a driver who has done the road before, which on this route is the point.
  • Private transfer Malpensa → Bormio — shorter than Livigno and a gentler road. The public alternative is train to Tirano then a valley bus.
  • An eSIM for Italy — note that Livigno sits close to the Swiss border and phones there like to hop onto a Swiss network. A data plan that covers Italy properly saves an unpleasant bill.
  • If your flight is delayed or cancelled — what EU rules pay, and what they do not.

Both towns run free ski buses, so once you are there a car is unnecessary — in Livigno the bus is practically part of the lift system.

Travelling with children

Bormio is the easier of the two with a family, for one reason: the baths. A week of skiing with children will include at least one day when somebody does not want to ski, and a thermal pool solves that day. The town also has normal shops and a walkable centre.

Livigno works well for families who ski every day — the free bus means no driving, and the duty-free prices take some sting out of a week's food and equipment. But the long, strung-out shape is a real factor with small children and ski gear. Look at a map before booking, and pick a room near a bus stop on the lift route.

The honest drawbacks

Livigno is remote, and the journey is the price of admission. Early season, December into mid-January, is the busiest and the most expensive. The two sectors not being linked by lift annoys people who expect to ski across a whole area. And the duty-free shopping draws day-trippers, which does not make the valley quieter.

Bormio suits intermediate and advanced skiers; the local area is not large and strong skiers will have covered it within a few days. Off-piste options are limited and the nightlife is modest. If your week is built entirely around skiing variety, this is the wrong valley.

Is it worth it in summer?

Livigno has over 150 km of marked walking paths, mountain biking and watersports on the lake, and stays genuinely cool — the average July daytime high is under 15 °C, which is either a feature or a problem depending on what you came for.

Bormio keeps the baths open year-round and sits at the foot of the Stelvio, where the glacier ski area usually runs from roughly June to November. The Stelvio pass road itself is one of the best-known climbs in Europe for cyclists.

Also worth sorting

  • Compare fares across airlines — Bergamo is often cheaper than Malpensa for this corner and no further away by road.
  • Left luggage in Milan — worth it if you add a city day rather than transferring straight up the valley.
