Region guide

Sicily

Big enough to be its own country, and it behaves like one. Greek temples, an active volcano, baroque hill towns and a coastline that takes a week to drive.

Where to base yourself

Sicily is too large for one base. Most people split a week or ten days between two, usually one on each coast.

Taormina, for the setting

A cliff town above the sea with Etna behind it and a Greek theatre with the best view in Italy. It is also the most expensive and the most visited place on the island, and the beach is a cable car ride below. Lovely for two or three nights, wearing after a week.

Ortigia, for the best of both

The old island heart of Siracusa, small enough to walk end to end, with honey-coloured baroque streets and the sea on every side. Quieter in the evening than Taormina, cheaper, and the natural base for the southeast.

Catania, for the practical trip

The airport, the car hire desks and the train station are all here, and rooms cost less than anywhere else on this list. The city itself is loud, black with lava stone and genuinely characterful — the fish market in the morning is the best thing in it.

Palermo, for the west

A different Sicily again: Arab-Norman churches, street food, markets that feel North African. Base here for Monreale, Cefalù and the Greek temples at Segesta.

Val di Noto, for the hill towns

Noto, Modica and Ragusa Ibla were rebuilt in one baroque style after the 1693 earthquake, and staying in one of them is the quietest version of the island. A car is essential here — more on that below.

Getting there and getting around

Catania or Palermo

Catania serves the east — Taormina, Etna, Siracusa and the Val di Noto. Palermo serves the west. If you are doing both halves, flying into one and out of the other saves a long drive back.

You need a car, and here is why

This is the least negotiable car recommendation in these guides. The trains follow the coast slowly and the interior has almost no service at all. The Greek temples at Agrigento and Segesta, the Val di Noto hill towns, the beaches worth the detour and the roads up Etna are all off any public network.

Driving in Sicily has a reputation, and the cities earn it — Palermo and Catania traffic is genuinely chaotic. Outside them the roads are quiet and often empty. The sensible pattern is to collect the car when you leave the city, not on the day you land.

The usual ZTL rule applies in historic centres: park outside the walls and walk in. Ortigia and the Val di Noto towns are strict about it.

Booking the practical bits

  • Rental cars at Catania — pickup at the airport for the eastern half of the island. Free cancellation on many, and summer sells out early.
  • Compare fares to Catania and Palermo — flying into one and out of the other is often no more expensive and saves a day of driving.
  • eSIM for Italy — you will be navigating constantly here, and mountain roads are not the place to find out your roaming is off.
  • Travel insurance for Italy — medical care, emergency transport, baggage and long delays.

Can you visit Sicily without a car?

Partly. You can have a good trip, but you will be seeing the cities and skipping the island.

What the trains and buses do cover

The coastal railway links Messina, Taormina–Giardini, Catania, Siracusa and on to Ragusa, and the line west reaches Palermo and Cefalù. Long-distance coaches are often faster than the train between the big cities and run frequently between Catania and Palermo. Taormina's station sits down at the shore with a bus or taxi up to the town.

What you will miss

Agrigento's Valley of the Temples is reachable but awkward. Segesta, the Val di Noto beyond Ragusa, the Etna roads, the salt pans near Trapani and nearly every good beach are not realistically reachable on public transport. Organised day trips fill some of the gap, especially for Etna.

A realistic plan without driving

Three nights in Ortigia, three in Palermo, moving between them by train or coach, with a day trip up Etna from Catania. That is a genuinely good week — it is just a city trip with a volcano attached rather than a Sicilian road trip.

Worth your time

The Greek theatre at Taormina, late in the afternoon, with Etna smoking behind the stage. Go an hour before closing when the coaches have gone.

Ortigia's market in the morning and the same streets empty at ten at night — it is two different towns in one day.

Etna from the north side. Quieter than the southern approach, and the lava fields look like another planet.

Spring and autumn beat summer. July and August are genuinely hot in the interior, and the island fills up.

Before you go

Book the car before the room for summer — the island runs out of rental cars before it runs out of beds. An eSIM and travel cover take a few minutes each before you fly.
