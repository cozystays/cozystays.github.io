Region guide

Amalfi Coast

Fifty kilometres of cliff with towns stacked up it. Spectacular, crowded in summer, and far easier if you arrive by boat rather than by car.

Which town to stay in

They are close together but they are not interchangeable. The choice is mostly about stairs, noise and budget.

Positano, for the view and the price

The one in every photograph: pastel houses poured down a ravine to a grey-sand beach. It is also the most expensive and the steepest — the town is essentially a staircase, and any room with the famous view is a long climb from the water. Beautiful, and exhausting with heavy luggage.

Amalfi, for convenience

Flatter, more workaday, and the ferry hub for the whole coast. Rooms cost noticeably less than Positano and you can reach everywhere else easily. Less photogenic, considerably more practical.

Ravello, for quiet

High above the coast, reached by a climbing road. Cooler in summer, far calmer in the evening, and the gardens at Villa Cimbrone and Villa Rufolo are the best thing on the coast. The trade-off is that you are committed to the bus or a taxi every time you want the sea.

Sorrento, for a cheaper base

Not technically the Amalfi Coast, but well connected by ferry and train, with more mid-range rooms. A reasonable compromise if the coast itself is beyond budget.

Getting there and getting around

Fly to Naples

Naples is the airport. From there the coast is roughly an hour and a half by road to Positano, longer in summer traffic.

Do not rent a car

This is the rare Italian region where a car is a liability. The coast road is single-lane in places, buses take the corners wide, parking is limited and costs more per day than many rooms. If you are staying on the coast and not touring further, skip it.

Move by boat instead

Ferries connect Sorrento, Positano, Amalfi and Salerno through the season. They are faster than the road in summer, cost little, and the coast looks completely different from the water — which is the real reason to take them. Services thin out considerably outside the summer months.

Booking the practical bits

  • Private transfer Naples airport → Sorrento — about 1 h 30 and 54 km. The alternative is the Circumvesuviana train, which is slow, crowded and has no room for suitcases.
  • Private transfer Naples airport → Positano — fixed price, driver meets you at arrivals. Much less stressful than the bus-and-ferry relay with luggage.
  • Boat tours along the coast — the coves you cannot reach by road, and no traffic.
  • Left luggage in Naples — 124 drop-off points from about €1.60 a bag a day, handy on the day you come back off the coast.

Worth your time

The Path of the Gods. A high ridge walk from Bomerano down to Nocelle above Positano, a few hours with the whole coast below you. Start early in summer; there is almost no shade.

The gardens at Ravello. Villa Cimbrone's terrace is the view people think of when they think of this coast, and it is quieter than anything at sea level.

An evening in Atrani. A ten-minute walk from Amalfi, almost nobody goes, and it is the last genuinely lived-in village on the coast.

Go in May, June or September if you have the choice. July and August are hot, full, and the road barely moves.

Where to stay on the Amalfi Coast without a car

This is the one region in these guides where arriving without a car is an advantage rather than a compromise. The coast road is single-lane in places, parking costs more per day than some rooms, and in August the traffic barely moves. Leaving the car at home is the right call.

Amalfi town is the base that works

It is flat by local standards, it is the hub for both the buses and the ferries, and rooms cost noticeably less than Positano. If you are not driving, everything gets easier from here.

Sorrento is the second choice: it has the train as well as the ferry, and more mid-range rooms. Positano works too, but the town is a staircase and the bus stops are up on the main road, so every arrival and departure involves steps with luggage. Ravello is beautiful and commits you to the bus or a taxi every single time you want the sea.

Getting there from Naples

The Circumvesuviana train to Sorrento is cheap and the locals use it, but it is slow, often crowded and has no real room for suitcases — fine with a backpack, unpleasant with a week's luggage. The alternative is a fixed-price transfer straight to your door.

Buses along the coast

SITA Sud runs the coast road: Sorrento–Positano–Amalfi, and Amalfi onward to Ravello and Salerno. Cheap and frequent in season, though you will often stand, and in high summer a full bus will sometimes pass a stop without taking anyone. Sit on the sea side if you get the choice.

Ferries are the better answer

Through the season, boats connect Sorrento, Positano, Amalfi and Salerno, with Capri from several of them. They are faster than the road in summer, cost little, and the coast looks completely different from the water — which is the real reason to take them. The important caveat: the ferries are seasonal. From roughly November to March the service thins out or stops, and the coast becomes a bus-only place.

Travelling with children

Be honest with yourself about this one: everything on this coast is stairs. Positano is essentially a staircase from the top road down to the beach, and a pram is close to useless there — most people end up carrying everything, including the child, several times a day.

If you are coming anyway, Amalfi town is the flat one and the ferry hub, which makes it the sensible base with small children. The ferries help for the same reason adults prefer them: faster than the coast road in summer, cheap, and nobody gets carsick on the hairpins. Atrani, a ten-minute walk from Amalfi, has a small sheltered beach and almost no crowd.

Before you go

Book accommodation early for summer — the towns are small and fill months ahead. An eSIM and travel cover take a few minutes each before you fly.
