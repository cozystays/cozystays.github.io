City guide

Rome

Most of what you came for sits inside a walkable square of about three kilometres. Which side of it you sleep on decides how the trip feels.

Which neighbourhood to stay in

Rome is not a city you need to see from a car or a bus. Pick a base inside the historic centre or one stop outside it, and walk.

Monti, for the balance

Between the Colosseum and Termini, and the best compromise in the city: old streets, small bars, residents who actually live there, and ten minutes on foot to the Forum. Rooms cost less than in the centro storico and the metro is close.

Trastevere, for the evening

Across the river, ivy and cobbles, and the liveliest place to eat after dark. The trade-off is noise — the lanes stay loud until well past midnight, so ask for a room off the street.

Centro storico, for the first visit

Pantheon, Navona, Campo de' Fiori. You pay for it, and the restaurants on the main squares are mostly not worth it, but you step out of the door into the Rome you pictured.

Prati, for quiet and value

North of the Vatican, orderly and residential, with good food and noticeably cheaper rooms. Twenty minutes' walk to the centre over the river, and the metro if you would rather not.

Near Termini, for the price

The cheapest rooms in the city, and the reason is the area. Fine if you arrive late or leave early; a dull base for four nights.

Getting there and getting around

Fiumicino or Ciampino

Fiumicino is the main airport, about 29 minutes and 27 km from the centre. Ciampino handles most budget flights and is closer but worse connected. The Leonardo Express from Fiumicino is quick, but it only goes to Termini — which is rarely where your room is, and the last leg with luggage is the part people underestimate.

You do not want a car

The whole centre is a ZTL, closed to non-resident traffic and camera-enforced, parking is scarce and expensive, and nothing you want to see is far enough apart to justify it. Rent one only on the way out, to continue into Tuscany or the south.

Walk, with the metro as backup

Two lines, which sounds thin and mostly is not: they cover the Vatican, the Spanish Steps, the Colosseum and Termini, and everything else is on foot. Buses are slow in traffic and worth skipping unless you are crossing the river late.

Worth your time

Book the Colosseum and the Vatican Museums ahead. Both sell timed entry, both sell out in season, and the queue without a ticket is hours rather than minutes. This is the one thing in Rome where planning genuinely changes the day.

Go early or go late. The Forum at opening and the Trevi Fountain before eight are different places from the ones in the afternoon photographs.

Eat one street back. The rule that holds everywhere in Rome: leave the square, take the first lane, and the same meal costs a third less and is better.

The Appian Way on a Sunday, when the old road is closed to traffic — tombs, umbrella pines and almost no one, twenty minutes from the centre.

Spring and autumn beat summer. July and August are hot enough to shorten the day considerably, and the queues are at their worst.

Before you go

Book accommodation early for spring and autumn — Rome fills for the long weekends more than people expect. An eSIM and travel cover take a few minutes each before you fly.
